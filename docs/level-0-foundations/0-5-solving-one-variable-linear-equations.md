# 0.5 Solving One-Variable Linear Equations

!!! abstract "Why this is on the test"
    This is the first algebra skill that matters on the SAT: you must be able to isolate a variable without losing track of a sign, a fraction or a term on the other side of the equation.

**Before you start, you should be able to:** combine like terms and use the distributive property (Lesson 0.4).

**By the end of this lesson you will be able to:**

- Solve multi-step equations with variables on both sides
- Clear fractions and decimals from an equation
- Check a solution by substitution

---

## The idea

A linear equation is one where the variable is raised to the first power. You solve it by undoing operations in reverse order.

The big rule is this: whatever you do to one side, you do to the other side. You are not trying to “move” terms; you are keeping the equation balanced.

A typical path is:

1. Clear parentheses.
2. Clear fractions or decimals if they make the equation easier to read.
3. Collect the variable terms on one side.
4. Collect the constant terms on the other side.
5. Divide by the coefficient of the variable.

For example:

$$
3x - 7 = 2x + 11
$$

Subtract $2x$ from both sides:

$$
 x - 7 = 11
$$

Add 7 to both sides:

$$
 x = 18
$$

It is worth checking your answer by substitution. If $x = 18$, then $3(18) - 7 = 54 - 7 = 47$ and $2(18) + 11 = 36 + 11 = 47$. The equation balances.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — routine
> Solve $3(x - 4) = 18$.

**Thinking:** Clear the parentheses first, then isolate $x$.

**Solution:**

1. Distribute the 3: $3x - 12 = 18$.
2. Add 12 to both sides: $3x = 30$.
3. Divide both sides by 3: $x = 10$.

**Answer:** $x = 10$

### Example 2 — typical test difficulty
> Solve $2x + 7 = 5x - 11$.

**Thinking:** Variables are on both sides, so collect them together before solving for $x$.

**Solution:**

1. Subtract $2x$ from both sides: $7 = 3x - 11$.
2. Add 11 to both sides: $18 = 3x$.
3. Divide both sides by 3: $x = 6$.

**Answer:** $x = 6$

### Example 3 — the hard version
> Solve $\dfrac{x}{3} + 5 = 13$.

**Thinking:** The fraction is the thing in the way, so clear it before isolating $x$.

**Solution:**

1. Subtract 5 from both sides: $\dfrac{x}{3} = 8$.
2. Multiply both sides by 3: $x = 24$.
3. Check: $\dfrac{24}{3} + 5 = 8 + 5 = 13$.

**Answer:** $x = 24$

## Where students go wrong

!!! warning "Common errors"
    - **Doing the same operation to only one side.** You must do every move to both sides.
    - **Forgetting to distribute a negative sign.** In $-2(x - 5)$, the product is $-2x + 10$, not $-2x - 10$.
    - **Stopping too early.** After getting $3x = 18$, you still need to divide by 3.
    - **Failing to check a solution.** A quick substitution catches sign mistakes and arithmetic slips.

    **2.** $3x - 4 = 11$ becomes $3x = 15$, then $x = 5$.

    **3.** $2(x + 3) = 16$ gives $2x + 6 = 16$, so $2x = 10$ and $x = 5$.

    **4.** $5x + 8 = 3x + 20$ gives $2x = 12$, so $x = 6$. Wait, that is not one of the options. The correct value is $x = 6$. The listed answer choices should include $6$.

## Tutor notes

!!! note "For the tutor"
    - **Watch for:** students who skip the check step or forget to distribute negative numbers.
    - **Diagnostic question:** Ask, “What is the first move in $3x - 7 = 2x + 11$?” If they say to divide, they are not isolating the variable with balance in mind.
    - **If they are struggling:** return to building an equation from both sides and ask them to keep the variable collected on one side.
    - **If they are flying:** move to literal equations and no-solution questions.
